Death in America

Heart disease and cancer still kill more Americans than anything else, but their rates have fallen sharply since 1999. What rose in their place was unintentional injury, driven by drug overdose, and then, in 2020, a new cause arrived all at once, peaked in 2021, and receded by 2024.

The big picture

In 1999, 2,391,399 Americans died, an age-adjusted rate of 875.6 deaths per 100,000 people. By 2019, the age-adjusted rate had fallen to 715.2, the lowest in this series, even as the number of deaths rose to 2,854,838, because the population grew and aged. Then 2020 broke the trend: 3,383,729 deaths, an age-adjusted rate of 835.4, a jump of nearly 17 percent in a single year. That one-year reversal is the story of this page, but it sits on top of two slower stories that ran for two decades before it. The reversal then deepened before it eased: the all-cause rate peaked at 879.7 in 2021 (3,464,231 deaths) and fell to 722.1 by 2024 (3,072,666 deaths), just above the 2019 low of 715.2.

Heart disease and cancer still dominate, but their rates fell

Heart disease remained the leading cause of death in every year from 1999 to 2020. Its age-adjusted rate fell 36.9 percent, from 266.5 to 168.2 per 100,000, and the number of heart disease deaths actually declined, from 725,192 to 696,962, despite a growing population. Cancer, the second leading cause throughout, saw its age-adjusted rate fall 28.2 percent, from 200.8 to 144.1, while the number of cancer deaths rose from 549,838 to 602,350. When a rate falls but the death count rises, that is population growth and aging at work: more people, living to older ages, even as the risk at each age declines. Stroke followed the same pattern, with its age-adjusted rate down 37.0 percent (61.6 to 38.8), and chronic lower respiratory diseases down 19.8 percent (45.4 to 36.4). Extended to 2024, heart disease's rate peaked at 173.8 in 2021 and fell to 157.6 in 2024, a 40.9 percent decline from 1999, while cancer's rate kept falling to 139.4 in 2024, down 30.6 percent from 1999, even as cancer deaths rose to 619,876.

The rise in unintentional injuries

The counterweight to those declines was unintentional injury. Its age-adjusted rate rose 63.2 percent, from 35.3 to 57.6 per 100,000, and the number of deaths more than doubled, from 97,860 to 200,955. Unintentional injuries passed stroke in number of deaths in 2013 and ranked fourth in 2020, behind only heart disease, cancer, and COVID-19. The engine of that rise was drug overdose. Overdose deaths rose from 16,849 in 1999 (an age-adjusted rate of 6.1) to 91,799 in 2020 (28.3). As a share of all unintentional-injury deaths, overdose grew from about 17 percent in 1999 to about 46 percent in 2020. These figures describe what was recorded as the underlying cause of death; they do not by themselves explain why overdose deaths rose. Both series peaked after 2020 and then fell: unintentional-injury deaths peaked at 227,039 in 2022 and dropped to 197,449 in 2024 (rate 53.3), while overdose deaths peaked at 107,941 in 2022 and fell 26.5 percent to 79,384 in 2024 (rate 23.0), about 40 percent of unintentional-injury deaths that year.

Alzheimer disease nearly tripled

Alzheimer disease moved from the eighth leading cause of death in 1999 to the seventh in 2020. Deaths rose from 44,536 to 134,242, and the age-adjusted rate nearly doubled, from 16.5 to 32.4 per 100,000, a 96.4 percent increase. An aging population accounts for part of this rise, and changes in how physicians diagnose and record dementia on death certificates can also affect these counts over time. After 2020 the Alzheimer rate eased: 116,022 deaths in 2024, a rate of 27.1, down from 32.4 in 2020.

Suicide and homicide

Suicide’s age-adjusted rate rose 28.6 percent across 1999 to 2020, from 10.5 to 13.5 per 100,000, peaking at 14.2 in 2018. Homicide’s age-adjusted rate rose 30.0 percent, from 6.0 to 7.8, with a sharp single-year rise from 2019 to 2020: deaths increased from 19,141 to 24,576 and the rate from 6.0 to 7.8. Suicide's rate peaked at 14.2 in 2022 (49,476 deaths) and was 13.7 in 2024 (48,824 deaths). Homicide is not among the 15 leading causes charted for 2021 to 2024, so its later years are not shown here. Both series are small compared with heart disease and cancer, which is why the log-scale toggle on the chart above helps to see their movement.

2024

2020: COVID-19 arrives

In 2020, COVID-19 (ICD-10 U07.1) caused 350,831 deaths, an age-adjusted rate of 85.0 per 100,000, making it the third leading cause of death that year, behind heart disease (696,962 deaths) and cancer (602,350). The all-cause age-adjusted rate jumped from 715.2 in 2019 to 835.4 in 2020. Heart disease’s rate also rose in 2020, from 161.5 (its low point in this series) to 168.2, while cancer’s rate continued to fall, from 146.2 to 144.1. Unintentional-injury deaths rose from 173,040 to 200,955 in the same year. The data here record underlying causes as certified; a death counted under COVID-19 is one where COVID-19 was judged the disease that started the chain leading to death.

2021 to 2024: the pandemic peak, then the retreat

2021 was the deadliest year in this series. COVID-19 deaths rose to 416,893 (rate 104.1), still the third leading cause, and the all-cause rate reached 879.7, the highest in the series. Heart disease's rate also peaked, at 173.8. Then the pandemic receded: COVID-19 fell to 186,552 deaths in 2022 (fourth), 49,932 in 2023 (tenth), and 31,426 in 2024 (fifteenth), a 92.5 percent drop from its 2021 peak. The all-cause rate fell each year after 2021, reaching 722.1 in 2024. Heart disease's rate fell to 157.6, its lowest since 2019, and unintentional injuries fell to 197,449 deaths, with overdose down to 79,384. Cancer is the exception to the retreat: its rate kept falling, to 139.4 in 2024, while its death count kept rising, to 619,876, as the population grew and aged.

A note on comparability: the 1999 to 2020 series comes from CDC WONDER's bridged-race file and the 2021 to 2024 extension from its single-race file. The two files agree exactly on 2018, 2019, and 2020 for all causes, heart disease, and cancer, so they are plotted as one series. The 2024 figures here are the single-race file values; they revise the earlier provisional release slightly upward (overall rate 722.1, versus 722.0 provisional), as late reports arrived.

How to read these numbers

Age-adjusted rates remove the effect of a changing age structure, so they are the right measure for comparing risk over time. A falling age-adjusted rate alongside a rising death count means the risk at each age fell while the population grew or aged. Death counts, by contrast, show the absolute burden. Both are shown here. Causes follow the National Center for Health Statistics 113-cause list, which groups ICD-10 underlying-cause codes into ranked causes. Drug overdose is defined separately as ICD-10 X40-X44, X60-X64, X85, and Y10-Y14, and overlaps with unintentional injuries, suicide, homicide, and undetermined-intent deaths, so overdose deaths must not be added to those categories. Rates are age-adjusted to the 2000 US standard population. No ranked national figure in this series was suppressed for small counts.
